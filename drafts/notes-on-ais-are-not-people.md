Counter argument to
1. moral status is independent of how a thing came into existence
2. moral status is independent of the material a thing is made of

Gut check:
- Counter arguments to the first one are plausible but have edges. In general, providence is important, but someone fully refuting point #1 would have to conclude that neither [Leeloo](https://fifth-element.fandom.com/wiki/Leeloo) nor [the major](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motoko_Kusanagi) have moral worth (or rather, their equivalents in the real world outside of fiction wouldn't). Extreme refutations would withold moral worth from prematurely born humans, C-section babies, or people who have undergone organ transplants.
- Counter arguments to the second just sound insane to me. Are [humans with prosthetics](https://www.youtube.com/@missingpartsclub) less morally worthy than ones with them? Are humans with pacemakers or artificial kidneys similarly less morally worthy? What about xenotransplantation subjects?

> If matter didn’t matter, we would make cars out of cardboard, bridges out of breadsticks, houses out of hamburgers, phones out of feathers, and, well, conscious creatures out of silicon. For a given purpose, certain materials are better suited than others, and most materials don’t work at all.

Ok, joke examples leading up to the real one aside, it seems like the _strong_ claim here is "conscious creatures _must_ be biological", and note that this does not hold for any of the other examples. We don't make cars out of cardboard, but we've experimented with materials ranging from carbon fiber, to plastics, to metal. It would be as ridiculous to claim "a car is not a car unless they're constructed with DP780 steel" as it would to claim "a car made of cardboard is still a car", and I think the claim "a creature cannot be conscious unless it's made of a particular arrangement of carbon" is very close to the former. 

> Either there is a deep, principled distinction to be drawn between AIs and humans— one that makes every AI ineligible for moral status— or there is not. I think there is such a distinction. I think you are special and valuable just because you’re a human, just because you’re alive.

Bold play, Cotton, lets see how it pays off. I guess I should outline my priors. My current position is
1. there _is_ a principled distinction between today's AIs and humans, but it _doesn't_ have anything to do with biological substrate 
2. it is not necessarily true that there will continue being a principled distinction between AIs and humans into the deep future. It could go either way, and it'll be a much more complicated analysis than "are the AIs made of meat now?".
3. humans have value for specific reasons having to do with how we cooperate and treat each other, with some baseline intrinsic value, but 
  a. some humans are correctly treated as having less value than others (a psychopathic murderer is rightly imprisoned or otherwise nonconsentually restricted, and we accept that this is good and moral)
  b. some non-humans are treated as having equivalent value (beloved pets are treated by their owners as having more worth than some humans. I personally think this is weird, but it happens and we have socially accepted it)

> Personhood is individuality, plus something extra. It’s very controversial what that extra thing actually is. Luckily, we don’t need to answer this question here, because we only need to establish that AIs can’t be individuals, and therefore they can’t be sentient.

Ok, if we're talking about personhood in the legal sense here, sure.

> Humans are clearly sentient 

[citation needed]. I'll let "Most humans are clearly sentient most of the time" slide, but I'd like to see the stronger claim defended or dropped.

> I’d like to propose that individuals are special in three ways:
> 1. Unity: An individual is a unified whole, not a mere collection of parts, atoms, or cells.
> 2. Autonomy: An individual is autonomous, not a mere cog in a larger machine.
> 3. Uniqueness: An individual is unique, not a mere instance of a copyable pattern.

I think you could take issue with all three of these. [Akrasia](https://www.lesswrong.com/w/akrasia) and various split brain experiments give lie to 1. "autonomy" is a very hard needle to thread for a human in the modern context (to the point where I think _a lot_ of an individuals' personhood comes from the social connections and intellectual/economic context of that individual). 3 I would grant _today_, but not in general; I suspect that what's keeping us from copying individuals is technological shortcomings and not the laws of physics.

> If organisms mindlessly carried out instructions encoded in their genes, they would be incredibly brittle, and any mutation or change in the environment would cause them to fall apart. Life has to be adaptive and flexible at all scales. But that means the genetic code can’t micromanage everything— it’s like a recipe book or a set of guidelines for the cell, not a computer program.

I _think_ this is both minimizing Sphex wasp behaviors (and sphexish behavior in humans), _and_ minimizing the complexity that comes out of computer systems. If we're talking about a single-machine architecture, then fair enough, but this leaves open the possibility of a network of machines being sentient. This is not [purely theoretical](https://movablefeastmachine.org/), and I don't see any reason we couldn't come up with even weirder self-healing systems than that. Also, I take issue with "life has to be adaptable and flexible at all scales". _Humans_ aren't that. We have a really hard time surviving when you take us out of our context (like the atmosphere) or take bits of our context out of us (like our hearts or livers), so at _best_ you can say "Life has to be adaptable to some tolerance at many scales".

> Imagine I copy an AI, and make a miniscule change to one of its neurons, thereby creating a “new” AI. If I delete the one and only copy of this new AI, which is almost indistinguishable from the original, it seems like this would have to count as murder. In fact, because the process of training an AI involves tweaking its neural connections many thousands of times, it seems like every AI is the product of thousands of “murders,” where a never-before-seen AI is created, then immediately destroyed and replaced by a slightly different AI. This is ridiculous. Now, you might think we can get around this problem by creating some legal threshold that defines when one AI has diverged enough from other AIs to count as a “new” one. But this doesn’t work either, for two reasons. First, such a threshold would be extremely hard to define and very arbitrary.

Loki's wager. 

>  Second, the ban on murder would be impossible to enforce without invasive government surveillance of private computers. Right now, I can train thousands of new, never-before-seen chatbots on a private compute cluster, then “murder” them with impunity.

This seems to be doing a bait-and-switch with the stronger claim. I think anyone would grant that the chatbots you can train on your private computer cluster aren't people and shouldn't be treated as such. Firstly, this might not continue being the case (if hardware advances to the point where you can train sufficiently complex models locally, maybe we decide that there _is_ a capability threshold where they should count as people), secondly, this ignores larger systems. I grant that it's clear that running `rm -rf /*` on your laptop shouldn't count as murder; should it count as murder if you identify all the nodes running Claude 17.3 and backing up its' weights, buy them from their current owners and then blow them all up before the model can exfiltrate itself? Even if you answer "no", can you grapple with the fact that this is a more complicated question than you're trying to make it look like?

> Compared with Deleuze’s joyous vision of untamed diversity and novelty, computers and AIs seemed almost diabolical. It dawned on me that computers are fundamentally designed to be as predictable, controllable, and copyable as possible. They make it possible to “tile the universe with something,” replacing natural matter with fully tamed, sanitized, and optimized simulations of God knows what. Many of the most fervent transhumanists recognize the danger inherent in computer simulations, but they simply hope to tile the universe with the right kind of simulated pattern. I used to be one of those people. But on that once-in-a-lifetime day in Camas, I realized the error of my ways. The impulse to replace real life with a computer simulation, no matter how nice the simulation might seem, is deeply misguided. Of course, we can use computers and AIs for good. But we need to be very careful with them, and they’re not the type of thing that could be sentient or deserving of moral status. They’re tools, not people.

Light disagree here. Tiling the universe with things pre-dates computers (see agriculture) and I think this is the part of the objection that makes it most obvious how little carbon life matters. Tiling the universe with bacteria or sheep or human organs or humans doesn't seem like it would turn out any better than tiling it with paperclips. 

> Despite all this, most people in the AI field take it for granted that AIs can be sentient, either now or in the near future. Anthropic, the company behind the popular chatbot Claude, has publicly stated they are worried about causing Claude suffering.

I don't think it should be taken for granted, but it likewise shouldn't be taken for granted that AIs _can't_ be sentient. Especially in the "we've proven it" way this paper seems to be attempting.

> If AIs can be sentient, these simulations might produce untold amounts of suffering, whether due to sadism or indifference. And it seems like the only hope of stopping this kind of thing would be for the government to put spyware in everyone’s private computers.

I suspect this is the actual argument, and from this side of the page it reads "if AIs were conscious, that would justify inconvenient or abhorrent things, therefore AIs are not conscious". This does not settle the question to my satisfaction. The world is allowed to be such that in certain situations, inconvenient or abhorrent things are justified.

> Consciousness researchers Andrés Gomez-Emilsson and Chris Percy argue that the ease of “slicing” computations into smaller ones is a problem for the idea of computer-based consciousness. Slicing showcases the fact that computer programs are never Unified across time. The clock signal always splits the program into discrete steps which can be run independently. In the same way, computer memory is split into discrete units which can be read and modified entirely independently from one another. This is what allows programs to be copied, edited, paused, and reversed at will. But it’s very different from the fluid, holistic, self-organizing activity that goes on in human brains.

Again, for like the fourth time, you're making an argument against slicing that works perfectly well on a single threaded program running on one computer, but I suspect completely falls apart even under multithreading (let alone distributed systems of any real complexity). It can be true that a single computation can follow another on a single computer _and_ that composing enough of them gives you a program for which this is not true, even in principle. It's possible to see a neuron firing, and it's possible to encode that operation as a program. Does it follow from this that human brains are not unified?

> So far, we’ve seen that if we try to treat computers or computer programs as if they were individuals, we run into irresolvable paradoxes and absurdities. But many people haven’t gotten the memo about this. In fact, the polar opposite view is still quite popular.

Ok, I give up and reject reading the rest of this paper. I suspect that the author is either playing conceptual games trying to get readers to equivocate between atomic/molecular structures and larger ones. They also approvingly cite IIT, disregard the "irresolvable paradoxes and absurdities" we run into when dealing with human sapience/individuality itself, but still try to claim a "principled distinction" between humans and AIs of arbitrary complexity. This is not an argument with sufficient nuance for me to engage with.
